**Abstract**: The animated film *Chang'an* depicts the tumultuous lives and deep friendships of poets such as Li Bai and Gao Shi, recreating the golden age of poetry during the prosperous Tang Dynasty. **Key words**: *Chang'an*; *Invitation to Wine*; Beauty of Sense; Beauty of Sound; Beauty of Form

The English subtitling of Chinese films plays an important role in the dissemination of Chinese culture. High-quality subtitle translation can break down language barriers, promote the international spread of Chinese films, and expand the global influence of Chinese culture. Classical Chinese poetry is regarded as a gem of Chinese culture, and Tang poetry is widely recognized as the pinnacle of Chinese classical poetry. Drawing on Tang Dynasty history, the film *Chang'an* tells the story of the legendary Tang poets Li Bai and Gao Shi and, through an ingenious narrative perspective, sketches a group portrait of the Tang Dynasty filled with historical weight and romantic poetic feeling. The film features 48 Tang poems in all, among which *Invitation to Wine* is the longest and the most magnificently heroic in spirit. This paper attempts to appreciate the English subtitles of *Invitation to Wine* in the film from the perspective of the celebrated poetry translation theory of the "Three Beauties."

## 1. The Emotional Implications of the Verses of *Invitation to Wine*

The title 将进酒 (*qiang jin jiu*, "Bring in the Wine") is an old title from the Han Yuefu. Historical records show that the poem was composed impromptu while Li Bai was drinking with Cen Xun and Yuan Danqiu. In the film, the poem unfolds Li Bai's world of imagination, hovering between the real and the illusory, crystallizing the freedom, unconventionality, romance, and bitterness he had distilled from more than forty years of life. By fully engaging the audience's visual and auditory senses, it conjures a sublime artistic vision: by the Yellow River, Li Bai's drinking spirits surge; he splashes wine that turns into water; then cranes fly forth, and Li Bai and his drinking companions rise upon them, dancing high in the air. They pass through the clouds and the deep pools beneath waterfalls that churn up a thousand heaps of snow, and rush toward the boundless galaxy; as they roam with the cranes, the Yellow River and the Milky Way merge at the horizon. The party rides the cranes into celestial palaces, where Li Bai, Gao Shi, and a host of immortals raise their cups and drink to their hearts' content, then soar to the ninth heaven, before finally plunging back into reality.

The lines of *Invitation to Wine* are uneven in length, intricate and staggered; its tempo shifts between swift and slow, sweeping forward like a rushing torrent. The emotion it expresses is profound and vigorous, of extraordinary scope. The feeling is at once bitterly indignant and wildly unbridled; the language is boldly unrestrained yet composed, soaring and plunging with dramatic intensity. Drowning his sorrows in wine, Li Bai laments the transience of life and the fleetingness of time, giving vent to his resentment at talents left unrecognized.

## 2. The English Subtitles of *Invitation to Wine* from the Perspective of the "Three Beauties Theory"

On the subject of writing, Lu Xun once proposed: "In learning a single character, one must know three things—form, sound, and meaning: recite it aloud so that the ear hears its sound, observe it so that the eye takes in its form, and ponder it so that the heart grasps its meaning; only when all three are employed together is the mastery of one character complete. In composition... there are thus three beauties: beauty of sense to move the heart—this is the first; beauty of sound to delight the ear—the second; and beauty of form to please the eye—the third." [1] Later, the scholar Xu Yuanchong wrote in his work *Literature and Translation*: "I applied Lu Xun's 'three beauties' to translation, and it became the 'three beauties' theory of verse translation." In his view, "verse translation should take beauty as its starting point, unifying the artistic conception, the music, and the form of the original poem, so as to convey, as far as possible, the distinctive charm of classical poetry for the reader's enjoyment." [2]

The English subtitles of *Invitation to Wine* in the film:

> Can you see the Yellow River decanting from the sky, / And racing to the sea never to return? / Can you see the grief of white hair in the mirrors, / And dawn's black silk turns to evening snow? / When life goes well, be joyous; / Never show the moon an empty cup. / Heaven gave me talent for a reason; / Spend now, riches return in season. / Stew the lamb, prepare the ox, let us feast; / Tonight we'll drink three hundred cups at least. / Master Cen, Master Danqiu, drink and don't put down your cups. / I'll sing for you; Please lend your ears. / Bells and jades are not so precious, / As eternal inebriation. / Ancient sages leave no name; / It's great drinkers who enjoy great fame. / At the table of Prince Chen of old, / Wine flowed like water. / Hosts shouldn't fret over coasts – / Buy more wine and fill those cups. / Fine horses, expensive robes – / Tell the servant to sell these for wine; / We'll banish sorrow from this world.

### (1) The Beauty of Sense in the English Subtitles of *Invitation to Wine*

In order to preserve the "imagery" and "artistic conception" of the source poem, the translator deliberated with great care and chose words with precision. For instance, "Yellow" in "Yellow River" retains the original color of 黄河 (the Yellow River); "decanting" in "decanting from the sky" conveys the majestic, spectacular picture of water pouring down from the heavens, and "racing to the sea" exhibits the surging momentum of the river. The line "the grief of white hair in the mirrors" is translated literally (明镜之中白发的忧伤, "the sorrow of white hair in the bright mirror"), and it seizes upon the source poem's theme of white hair mirrored in the looking glass. In "dawn's black silk turns to evening snow?", the term "black silk" is truer to reality than 青丝 (black tresses of youth), and its vivid contrast with 白雪 ("evening snow") conveys the mercilessness of the years and the brevity of life. The line "When life goes well, be joyous" is plain and accessible, conveying the author's appeal to treasure happy times and enjoy the moment. "Never show the moon an empty cup" preserves the images of 月 (the moon) and 樽 (the cup), opens up reflections on not letting good hours go to waste, and passes on to the reader a grain of everyday wisdom. In "Heaven gave me talent for a reason," "Heaven" stands for 上天 (the Heaven above), stressing that talent is bestowed with a purpose. "Spend now, riches return in season" conveys the cyclical return of wealth and encourages a positive outlook. The line "Stew the lamb, prepare the ox, let us feast" abounds in rich visual and gustatory imagery, depicting the grandeur, luxury, and liveliness of the banquet. "drink three hundred cups" corresponds to 三百杯 ("three hundred cups") in the source text, conveying a scene of exuberance and deep drinking. "Bells and jades" stands for precious objects, yet they count for less than 长醉 ("eternal inebriation"). "Ancient sages leave no name" expresses the lonely plight of the sages of old. "Wine flowed like water" vividly conveys the abundance of wine and the flow of drinking, projecting a strong image of wine and accentuating the atmosphere of merriment and feasting. "fill those cups" conveys the intent to keep the cups brimming. "Fine horses" and "expensive robes" refer to fine steeds and splendid robes. "banish sorrow from this world" conveys the host's wish to drown his cares in generous draughts of fine wine. Through its many images and conceptions, the rendering as a whole expresses a bold, unrestrained attitude and atmosphere.

### (2) The Beauty of Sound in the English Subtitles of *Invitation to Wine*

When translating poetry, the translator must strive to preserve the music and the outward form of the source poem. Yet the phonology, the sound–script structures, and the rhyming conventions of different languages differ greatly; in actual practice, the translator is sometimes obliged to compromise in order to balance the artistic conception, the music, and the form of the poem as a whole. In respect of music, the rhymes in the first four lines of the subtitles—"see"—"sky," "decanting"—"racing"—"return," and "hair"—"black"—may not strike the ear as forcibly as those of the source poem, yet the translator's ingenuity is still perceptible. In rhythm, each line of the rendering by and large keeps a cadence close to that of the source poem, with no sentences overly long or overly short; this balanced rhythm makes the poem read all the more fluently. In 人生得意须尽欢，莫使金樽空对月 ("When life goes well, be joyous; never show the moon an empty cup"), "be joyous" for 须尽欢 and "never" for 莫 restore the imperative mood of the source, matching it in rhythm and feeling alike. In "Heaven gave me talent for a reason; / Spend now, riches return in season," "reason" and "season" rhyme, giving a strong sense of rhythm. "Stew the lamb, / prepare the ox, / let us feast." breaks the single full line 烹羊宰牛且为乐 of the source poem into three short clauses—烹羊 ("stew the lamb"), 宰牛 ("prepare the ox"), 且为乐 ("let us feast")—and the tempo suddenly turns brisk, as if one could hear the feasters beating out a rhythm on their instruments. "Bells and jades are not so precious / As eternal inebriation." merges two lines into one, with the euphony residing in the three words "bells"—"jades"—"precious." "Ancient sages leave no name; / It's great drinkers who enjoy great fame." rhymes unmistakably, as in "name"—"fame" and "sages"—"drinkers." "Fine horses, expensive robes – / Tell the servant to sell these for wine; / We'll banish sorrow from this world." brings the poem to a close. Given the difficulty of the source poem, the author has here adopted literal translation; as with the preceding lines, some of the flavor of the original seems attenuated, yet the author still does his utmost to preserve the rhythm and rhyme of the source text, as shown in "robes"—"horses" and "servant"—"sell."

### (3) The Beauty of Form in the English Subtitles of *Invitation to Wine*

Owing to the special nature of subtitle translation, the translator must give particular consideration to the "beauty of form" of the rendering. In the subtitling of *Invitation to Wine*, it is not hard to see that the translator has invested great effort in the "form" of the text. In most of its lines, the English version corresponds fairly closely to the source poem in structure and cadence, and some lines (e.g. "Stew the lamb, / prepare the ox, / let us feast; / Tonight we'll drink three hundred cups at least.") have been appropriately adjusted in translation to foreground the scenes of feasting and drinking, so as to suit the needs of subtitling. Moreover, since the time and space available to subtitles are limited, words bearing cultural background (such as 高堂 "high halls," 五花马 "dappled horses," and 千金裘 "furs worth a thousand gold") and historical allusions (such as 陈王 "Prince Chen" and 宴平 "the banquet at Pingle") are omitted or rendered literally, to meet the spatiotemporal demands of subtitling and the comprehension needs of cinema audiences.

## 3. Conclusion

An analysis of the English subtitles of the poem *Invitation to Wine* in the film *Chang'an* from the perspective of the "Three Beauties Theory" shows that "beauty in sense," "beauty in sound," and "beauty in form" are interfused in the rendering. The English subtitles convey eight or nine out of every ten images of the source poem—for instance, the majestic spectacle of the waters of the Yellow River, the sorrow of sighing over fleeting time before the mirror, the affirmation of and confidence in one's own abilities, the extravagance and exhilaration of singing over wine at the banquet, and the open-handed scorn for money as mere dust. Secondly, the rendering strives to match the source poem in music and rhythm, employing numerous rhymes, such as reason—season, feast—least, name—fame, and coasts—cups—robes. The poem's rhythm is compact and forceful; its brisk, heroic tempo endows the poem with vitality and intense feeling. The verse is varied in sentence pattern—questions, statements, and imperatives enrich the rhythm of the whole poem. In terms of "form," given the requirements of film subtitles and audiences' comprehension needs, the lines mostly adopt colloquial short phrases and strive to match the film's visuals and the lip movements of its characters. On the whole, "beauty in sense," "beauty in sound," and "beauty in form" merge with and set one another off, jointly presenting the audience with a sense of beauty.

It should be noted that in the process of subtitling poetry into English, it is very difficult to do full justice to "imagery," "music," and "form" all at once. For example, the rendering omits the image of 高堂 ("high halls"); in conveying the image of 金樽 (the golden goblet), the translator retains only 樽 (the cup) and discards the "golden"; and the author refrains from elaborating on the three figures rich in background and cultural import—岑夫子 (Master Cen), 丹丘生 (Master Danqiu), and 陈王 (Prince Chen)—or on the historical allusion of 宴平 (the banquet at Pingle). Compared with the misty, veiled mood of the source line 但愿长醉不复醒 (would that I might stay forever drunk and never wake again), the rendering ("As eternal inebriation") is overly plain and direct in manner. Alongside the source poem, the rendering appears to fall short in the capture of flavor, the depth of emotion, the introduction of cultural background, and the precision of diction—yet these very "shortcomings" precisely embody the translator's wisdom in weighing "beauty in sense," "beauty in sound," and "beauty in form" when rendering this *Invitation to Wine*.
